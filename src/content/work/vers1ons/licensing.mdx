<BodySection label="Problem" heading="A license is only worth something if everyone can trust it, and ours couldn't yet be trusted.">
  What a licensor set on a listing didn't always match what the system allowed, so a remix could reach a store the license didn't cover. Once a remix was remixed again, it got hard to tell who should be credited and paid. A release that breaks its license is a legal problem for vers1ons, and the license is what we sell.
</BodySection>

<Tabs label="Three parts">
  <Tab id="listings">
    <BodySection label="What I did" heading="I led the product decisions on licensing and built it with our engineering team.">
      I worked with legal on the agreement, with labels and artists on the terms, and with our founders on the license model. We turned the agreement into data the platform runs on. Every purchase issues a rights grant that points back to the one it came from, and the terms a buyer signs are frozen at purchase. The listing wizard was shared work, and the parts below were mine.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={licensingListings} />

    <Row label="Contributors" heading="Everyone who's paid, in one list." step={4}>
      I built the contributors step with an inline split editor, merging songwriting and recording into one list, and made publishers payable parties with their own ID and a cut of their writer's share. A listing can't go live until every paid contributor has a phone number and a confirmed notice, and Review links back to what's missing.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Under the hood · Release check" heading="One rule decides where a remix may release." step={6}>
      Licensors choosing the stores a remix may go to was my feature, and I wrote most of the rule behind it. It's enforced at submit and again at delivery, which drops excluded stores at the last step, and the wizard shows its answer.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Under the hood · Locked once live" heading="Once it's live, what buyers relied on can't change." step={10}>
      A published listing freezes its license scope, title and audio, its codes can be set only once, and price and royalties stay editable. Saving a draft no longer writes license terms nobody chose, or turns the filename into the contract title.
    </Row>

  <Tab id="drops">
    <BodySection label="What I did" heading="I built Drops end to end: a remix contest where no one leaves as a loser.">
      Most remix contests run on a form and a Dropbox link. Stems leak, entries have no license, and the winning remix can't be released. In a drop, the license is the entry fee. I wrote the spec, designed the pages and wrote most of the drop code, 185 commits. It's shipped end to end, with the first drops being set up with artists now.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={licensingDrops} />

    <Row label="The rules, in public" heading="Every producer agrees to the exact rules they saw." step={3}>
      The artist is paid for every license sold, and every producer leaves licensed to release their remix. Agreeing to the rules used to only unlock a button. Now the server refuses an entry without it and records the time and a hash of the exact wording, so editing the rules later can't change what someone agreed to.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Under the hood · Stems" heading="Stems only reach the people who bought them." step={2}>
      Stems used to stream in full from the public page. Now only the owner, the managing label or a buyer gets full audio, and everyone else gets the 30-second preview. The check runs before the response is built, so an unentitled page never contains the links, and a missing preview means no audio, never the full song. Search and browse use a batched check, two queries whatever the page size.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Under the hood · Windows" heading="The windows are enforced on the server." step={5}>
      A drop that hadn't opened could still sell. Now the check runs where a purchase becomes real, creating the license and the payment, because the cart lives in the browser and can reach checkout weeks later. Release terms are frozen in the same transaction as the sale, and early-access codes are claimed there too, so two people can't use one at once. Delivery checks the release date itself, now being rolled out.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Judging" heading="The host plays, stars and picks." step={11}>
      A winners shelf in star order, All, Starred and Unheard filters, waveforms, markers for what's been heard, review by keyboard, and winners announced with a confirmation. Emails cover the waitlist code, the opening and "your drop is live".
    </Row>

  <Tab id="sheets">
    <BodySection label="What I did" heading="A satellite app that gets the paperwork done before a license exists.">
      Most co-written songs have no split sheet, and without one there's no clean license to sell. So I built sheets.vers1ons.com on my own, from nothing, 82 commits and almost all of it. Anyone can make a split sheet for free with no account. It's live as the first vers1ons satellite app.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={licensingSheets} />

    <Row label="No account" heading="Nothing is saved until you ask." step={1}>
      The sheet lives in the browser, checked against the expected format before it's loaded back, and the PDF is made without touching the database or storage. That route is rate-limited, and refuses if the limiter is down. Saving takes a free account, one sheet each, and a database rule means a double click, two tabs or a retry can't make a second.
    </Row>

    <Row label="Splits" heading="Splits balance the same way everywhere." step={7}>
      The split rules are shared with the main app, so Sheets and the vers1ons wizard balance splits the same way. The PDF can't be downloaded until both sides add up to 100%.

<BodySection label="The rule I broke" heading="I fell in love with the solution, not the problem.">
  My rule is to fall in love with the problem, not the solution. With Bespoke I did the opposite. The real problem was artisans connecting with the people who'd buy their work. Both versions chased an idea that looked great, without checking that anyone wanted it.
</BodySection>

<Tabs label="Then and now">
  <Tab id="bespoke-ai">
    <BodySection label="The idea" heading="Pick what you like, and an artisan makes it.">
      The first Bespoke started with one line. A ring, for my engagement in Lake Tahoe, for my artsy fiancée. It made nine images and you X'd out the ones you didn't like. Between rounds it asked questions like "Do you not like silver?" and kept a list of what you wanted and didn't, which you could edit. Every answer went back into the prompt. When you found the one, you sent it out and artisans bid to make it.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={bespokeAi} />

    <Row label="What I was thinking" heading="React to images instead of writing a brief." src={insight} alt="Do you not like silver? over the ring grid, with I like this, idk, and Not my vibe.">
      People know what they like when they see it, even when they can't describe it. So instead of writing a brief, you'd type one line and react to images. Every X and every answer sharpened the prompt until you found the one.
    </Row>

    <Comparisons label="Wireframe to design" intro="The wireframes I started from, and the screens they became." />

    <Figure src={artisan} alt="Bespoke AI for artisans on a laptop: I create unique rings for weddings for creative people." video="/media/bespoke/v1-artisan.mp4" caption="The artisan's side. Say what you make, pick a request, and bid on it (0:27)" />

    <Row label="Why it wouldn't work" heading="It took the artisan out of the design." side="left" src={bid} alt="An artisan's bid of $400 on a Pink Garnite Flower Ring, generated by a buyer.">
      Artisans make the things they enjoy making, and then sell them. Sometimes a client is part of the process, but the design is still theirs. Bespoke AI handed them a buyer's finished idea and asked them to build it, which takes away the creative freedom that makes their work worth buying. And the images came from AI, which knows nothing about an artisan's materials, tools, or limits. It wasn't realistic to expect a piece that looked like the picture, or even close.
    </Row>

    <Figure layout="center" src={fork} alt="The Fork slide: two options, Creative Tool for Users and Empower Artisans Through Digital Authentication." caption="The fork that led to the second version, from our deck" />
  </Tab>

  <Tab id="artisans">
    <BodySection label="The idea" heading="Start from artisans who already have a style.">
      The second Bespoke built for the other side. Artisans who already make high-end work list it, and buyers find it. Each piece gets an NFC tag before it's sold. Scanning the tag opens its digital passport, with the maker, the piece, and every owner since. When it sells, ownership moves to the buyer through a smart contract, and a share of every resale goes back to the maker and earlier owners.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={bespokeArtisans} />

    <Row label="What I was thinking" heading="Handmade becomes the luxury." src={value} alt="Value Proposition slide: digital passports in users' wallets, stress-free second-hand purchases, and artisans earning on every resale.">
      As more things get made by machines, handmade work becomes what people pay more for. Many artisans already have a style and a following. What they don't have is a way to prove a piece is theirs. An NFC tag on each piece gives it a passport. A smart contract sends the maker a share every time it resells.
    </Row>

    <MobileDesign intro="The flow on a phone, from Discover to the wallet. Click any screen to see it full size." />

    <Comparisons label="Wireframe to design" intro="The wireframes I started from, and the screens they became." />

    <Row label="Why it wouldn't work" heading="The blockchain asked too much of buyers." side="left" src={lifecycle} alt="A loop from artisan onboarding and RFID shipment through product listing, purchase, escrow, delivery, and resale.">
      The marketplace was the closest we got. Established artisans did want more people to find their work. But NFC tags, NFT twins, and smart contracts asked buyers to adopt something people don't use in everyday life. We also built the resale loop before anyone had made a first sale. A certificate of origin would have done the job.
    </Row>
  </Tab>

  <Tab id="today">
    <BodySection label="Start from the problem" heading="Help artisans increase their sales and get discovered.">
      Today I'd start with the problem we skipped. Artisans with great work and their own style need more people to find them. So Bespoke would be storefronts for artisans and a discover feed of real pieces you scroll like Pinterest. You could search with a picture of something you like, say a mid-century chair, and find the artisans who make that kind of work. The AI would read their real work, not generate pieces that don't exist.
    </BodySection>

    <SystemFigure label="How it works" spec={bespokeToday} />

    <BodySection label="What it would do" heading="Four things, for artisans and the people who buy from them.">
      **A storefront for each artisan.** Their pieces, their story, and how they make their work, in one place they can share. They keep designing what they enjoy making, and they can open commissions when they want to.

      **A discover feed.** Real pieces from many makers, browsed like Pinterest. Starting with one category, like ceramics, so the feed is worth scrolling from day one.

      **Search by image.** Add a photo of something you like, and see the artisans whose real work looks like it. You buy a piece they've made, or start a conversation about one they'd want to make.

      **A certificate of origin.** Every piece ships with one, made by the artisan in a few minutes. Buyers can check it with a code, with no wallet or app to learn.
    </BodySection>

    <Row label="What I'd keep" heading="The discover feed." src={phones} alt="The second version on three phones: Discover, a feed of real pieces, between a collection and a piece.">
      Browsing real work by the people who made it was the best part of the second version. Today it's the center of the product, and search by image is just another way into it.
    </Row>

    <Row label="What I'd change" heading="A certificate, not a blockchain." src={lifecycle} alt="The second version's passport loop, from artisan onboarding to resale.">
      Each piece would ship with a certificate of origin and an authentication package the artisan puts together in minutes. It proves where a piece came from without asking buyers to learn anything new.
    </Row>

    <Row label="What we never asked" heading="Whose problem, and which category." src={summary} alt="Summary slide: from sustainable materials sourcing, to a marketplace with creative AI tools, to a marketplace for digitally authenticated goods.">
      We never decided whether the problem was artisans finding buyers or buyers not valuing handmade work. Ceramics, shoes, clothing, paintings, and furniture each sell, ship, and price differently, and we designed for all of them at once. Today I'd start with one category.

      I also designed for how I thought people would buy in ten years and hoped they'd catch up. It had to work for how they buy today.
    </Row>

    <BodySection label="How I'd build it" heading="Build it, and let artisans tell me what's next.">
      In 2024 Bespoke stayed in Figma. Since then, working on vers1ons and in my internships, I've learned to use AI as leverage to build and to learn. Now I'd build a working version of this, put it in front of a few artisans in one category, and let what they and their buyers do decide what comes next.
    </BodySection>

    <BodySection label="What still holds" heading="Handmade will matter more.">
      One-of-one handmade work will matter more as more things are made by machines, and buyers want to know where things come from. That read hasn't changed.
    </BodySection>
  </Tab>
</Tabs>
